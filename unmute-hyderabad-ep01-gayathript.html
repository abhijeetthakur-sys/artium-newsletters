Three musicians on the floor of a brick-walled room in Hyderabad, playing a Carnatic composition the way it is meant to be played.

  ✦  

Right before she began, she was thinking about Lord Krishna. That is where her thoughts usually go before a song like this one. A peacock. Krishna. And then the raga itself. "The raga itself is Vrindavani," she says. "So that's it."

She has been performing this thillana in concerts for years. Choosing it for Hyderabad was a deliberate call. "This is one of the ragas which bring joy, happiness, and very energy. It's energetic. So I wanted to present this composition to bring out that vibe."

Carnatic music usually lives in a sabha. A kutcheri has its own shape — you open with a varnam, you go authentically into the big ragas, and the room knows exactly what it came for. Ameyaa Social was not that room. There were film songs on either side of her set, and an audience that had not necessarily come for classical music. She read that and picked accordingly. "If it is a very joyful moment and the atmosphere is mixed, then we can catch up with such compositions like thillana and bhajan kind of music, which will immediately set into any kind of atmosphere."

There is one passage in the composition she keeps returning to. She loves it enough that she will sing it again and again in a performance, intensifying the feeling each time it comes back around. If you watch closely, you can see where it lands.

Her guru taught her one thing about performing that she still carries: if you are having fear, you don't sing. Over the years she turned it into something more practical. If the song is joyful and the singer is not, the audience will read it instantly and the whole thing collapses. "When I keep the fear aside and start enjoying the composition, it transforms. It catches the vibe from the audience too. Audience take my vibe and I take their vibe. So that becomes joyful." When she finished, she says, she was somewhere else entirely — joyful, peaceful, in another trance world.

Brindavani Thillana

Composed by Dr. M. Balamuralikrishna, set to Vrindavani ragam. A thillana Gayathri has carried through years of concert performance and chose to open with here, against the grain of what a sabha programme would expect. Her reason was simple: it is one of the ragas that brings joy, happiness and energy, and that is what she wanted in the room. In her own words — "I wanted to present this composition to bring out that vibe."

 

"I imagine a peacock, I imagine Lord Krishna. And the raga itself is Vrindavani."

P.T. Gayathri

A note from the Talent Department

Hyderabad was our fourth city, and this is the first episode from that night to go up. It could have been a film song. We led with a Carnatic thillana instead.

What I keep coming back to is the choice. Gayathri could have opened the way a sabha expects. She read the room instead, picked a piece built to carry joy into a mixed audience, and the room went with her. That judgement is the thing worth noticing. It is not about how much you know. It is about knowing what a room can hold.
