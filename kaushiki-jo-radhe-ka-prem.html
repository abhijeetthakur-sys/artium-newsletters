Before You Press Play

A bhajan about Radha and Krishna that quietly argues they were never separate from Ram and Sita — that it is one love, wearing different names. Jo Radhe Ka Prem was written, composed and produced by Kaushiki Guha Thakurta, who drew the cover art as well.

It released on 22nd August 2026, during Jhulan Yatra — the festival that celebrates the divine love and togetherness of Radha and Krishna. Streaming worldwide.

 

Kaushiki Guha Thakurta

Singer • Composer • Lyricist • Teacher Series

Where the song came from

There was no plan. One night she was thinking about the leela of Krishna and Radha — their love, their connection, the divine emotion that sits between them. The thought stayed with her, and almost instinctively she picked up a pen and started scribbling these lines.

The philosophy arrived before the words did. “The words simply came as an expression of what I was feeling in that moment,” she says. “In a way, the thought found its words naturally, and later the music allowed those words to breathe.”

She grew up inside this material. Since childhood she has heard the stories of Krishna, Radha, Ram and Sita, and grown up on the bhajans, horis and compositions that celebrate those relationships. Somewhere along the way she realised that beyond the different stories and forms, one emotion stays constant — and that there is something very aesthetic about it. Not merely devotion, and not merely romance: tenderness, surrender, longing, joy.

 

About the Song

Radha’s love, in this song, is not only an emotion — it becomes part of Krishna’s leela, something so pure that love itself turns divine. And the song refuses to stop there: Ram and Sita are folded into the same thought, until four names and four sets of stories resolve into one. Kaushiki is clear that she is not trying to settle the argument. “This song is less about defining what Radha’s love is and more about experiencing it — just allowing yourself to be immersed in the music and letting that divine love speak for itself.”

She had a picture of the melody in her head, and then let the room change it. Pranay Chatterjee on tabla and Rick Mukherjee on flute brought their own ideas, and some of those turned out beautifully — especially towards the laggi. “There was a beautiful exchange of ideas throughout the process,” she says. She calls the finished song the fruit of a combined effort.

 

Jo Radhe ka prem hain Krishna ki leela
Wahi dono ek hi avatar
Ram bane swara Krishna bansuria
Sita pati hai Ram

Radha’s love is Krishna’s leela — the two are one and the same being. Ram becomes the note, Krishna the flute; and Sita’s lord is Ram.
 

Prem hain ek darshan
Wo chitta ka hain nivedan
Dil hain ek darpan
Wo jeevan ka sangram

Love is a way of seeing — the heart’s own offering. The heart is a mirror, and that is the struggle of a life.

The line the whole song turns on is the one it is named for — Jo Radhe ka prem hai, Krishna ki leela. Radha’s love is Krishna’s play.

She describes it as the power of love between divinity, and says it is not really something to be explained. “It is something you can simply feel through the music. When you sing it, there is a sense of surrender, longing, tenderness and devotion that flows naturally through the melody.”

 

“This song came from a simple thought — that while we worship Rama, Krishna, Radha or Sita through different stories, the love that binds them is the same. I wanted the music to reflect that feeling of oneness.”

Kaushiki Guha Thakurta
 

In the studio

The song was recorded at Kusum, Kolkata, mixed and mastered by Gautum Kundu. Kaushiki carried the release herself — writing, composing, producing — and describes the process as anything but heavy. “It was honestly a very joyful experience. It never felt stressful; in fact, it was a lot of fun, exciting, and a beautiful learning process for all of us.”

“Everyone involved simply gave themselves to the music,” she says. “We listened to each other, learned along the way, and encouraged one another to give our very best.” Looking back, she says it feels less like a responsibility than a beautiful experience they were fortunate to share.

The first person she played it to was her father. His opinion means a great deal to her, and she took his ideas and suggestions while the song was still being developed — so his perspective is inside the finished record. That is not incidental. Bhakti reached her through him first: Maa Durga has been worshipped in her family for generations, and this year marks 151 years of that tradition. She grew up watching him worship, chant mantras, and immerse himself completely in the ritual.

The harmonium and the notebook — inside the Jo Radhe Ka Prem sessions at Kusum, Kolkata

 

About Kaushiki

Kaushiki Guha Thakurta teaches at Artium and releases through the Teacher Series. She is a disciple of Pandit Ajoy Chakraborty and Shree Anol Chatterjee, and has received training in Hindustani classical music, thumri, ghazal and tappa. She completed her Sangeet Visharad in Hindustani classical vocal music at Chandigarh’s Pracheen Kala Kendra, and is a scholar at the Rajya Sangeet Academy under the Ministry of Cultural Affairs.

She performs on morning television — Kolkata Doordarshan among other channels — and in recitals and baithaks in India and abroad.

She is careful about where the melody came from. The raagas, she says, have been part of her since childhood, quietly woven into the way she listens and feels; even now, listening to her gurus, she finds something new each time. “Whatever little I have been able to absorb from their shikshya — and the deeper language of raaga — naturally finds its way into my music.” She will not claim this melody for any one influence. “Jo Radhe Ka Prem is a very humble output of the blessings and teachings I have received from my gurus.”

A Note from the Talent Development Department

Kaushiki carried this one end to end — words, composition, production, and the cover art. When I asked her what that was like, she did not describe it as a weight. She described it as fun: a beautiful learning process, everyone giving themselves to the music, everyone encouraging everyone else to give their best. That is a teacher’s answer, and she is a teacher.

What stayed with me is who heard it first. Not a label contact and not a playlist editor — her father. The same man she grew up watching worship Maa Durga, in a family tradition 151 years old this year. She took his notes while the song was still forming, so his ear is in the finished record. The Teacher Series exists for exactly this: music that is carried privately, learned at home, shaped by one family’s practice — and would otherwise never leave the room it was made in.

Abhijeet Thakur
