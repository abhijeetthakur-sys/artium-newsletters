Before You Press Play

A Hari bhajan with a plain argument underneath it: the things we hold on to are temporary, and the name is not. Hari Hari Naam Japo Re was composed and written by Dr. Bappa Dutta — who is also Paramita Roy’s Music Theory teacher — and produced by Pankaj Paul at A P Music Studio, Kolkata.

It released on 3rd September 2026, streaming worldwide. The chorus behind her is sung by Sreeja Mukherjee, Mrinalini Sengupta and Trisha Maiti — all three of them her students. On a song about which companions stay and which leave, that is not a small detail.

 

Paramita Roy

Singer • Teacher • Teacher Series

Where the song came from

This is Paramita’s second piece of work with Dr. Bappa Dutta, and the relationship runs both ways — he is also her Music Theory teacher. “I’ve had the privilege of knowing him not only as a composer but also as a teacher.” Because she already understood how he thinks musically, approaching his composition as a singer was a different kind of task. “It was a very special experience to approach his composition as a singer and try to bring out the emotion and bhakti in the song.”

The refrain settled the register before anything else did. It addresses the listener directly — bhai sadho. “It doesn’t feel like I’m simply performing a bhajan to an audience; it feels more like I’m speaking to someone directly and sharing a spiritual thought with them.” That changed how she sang it: “I had to approach it with a sense of sincerity and inner reflection, rather than just focusing on the musical aspects.”

 

About the Song

The whole song is built on one repeating line, and it is addressed to someone rather than performed at them. Everything else in it — happiness, companionship, wealth — is set up only to be let go of, so that the refrain can come back.

 

Hari Hari Naam japo re bhai sadho
Hari Hari Naam japo
Hari Naam me sab sukh paave
Wahi naam tapo re bhai Sadho

For a devotional song it does not flinch. One couplet says plainly that the friends of good times arrive in good times, and are gone when sorrow comes.

 

Sukh ke sathi sukh me aate
Dukh me chale jate hain

Ask Paramita whether she has lived that line and she does not soften it. “I’ve also experienced how some people are present when everything is going well, but when circumstances become difficult, their presence changes. It can be painful, but at the same time, those experiences teach us to understand relationships more deeply.”

What she values is that the bhajan does not stop at the grievance. “It doesn’t simply complain about that reality. It gently reminds us that worldly relationships can be temporary, while our spiritual connection and faith can give us a deeper sense of stability.”

 

Dhan daulat ki moh na karo
Jo kuchh hai khush usi me raho
Guruo ke guru hai wo
Kaal hai paapi un se daro

Guruo ke guru places Hari above even a teacher — a pointed thing to sing when your own teacher wrote the line. She sees no contradiction in it. “I see the guru as the person who guides us towards that higher truth... A guru doesn’t necessarily stand apart from devotion to Hari; rather, the guru becomes a pathway through which that devotion becomes deeper and more meaningful.” And then, simply: “So that line has a very special meaning for me.”

 

“I wanted to keep the message of this song very simple. Life changes, people come and go, and the things we are attached to don’t always stay with us. But the remembrance of Hari can remain with us through everything.”

Paramita Roy
 

In the studio

What she noticed most about Pankaj Paul’s production was the room he left in it. “He was able to retain the devotional essence of the bhajan while giving it a contemporary musical texture. The arrangement never overpowered the composition or the lyrics.” “There is a certain simplicity and spaciousness in the arrangement that allowed the words and the melody to breathe. As a singer, that gave me the freedom to express the devotional feeling of the piece without feeling restricted by the production.”

Sreeja, Mrinalini and Trisha are her students, and they sang the chorus. “Since the song speaks about relationships, companionship and the people who stay with us, having my students sing the chorus felt quite symbolic in its own way.” “It wasn’t just about adding more voices to the track; it felt like sharing the music and the experience with the next generation of learners.”

Recording the chorus — A P Music Studio, Kolkata

 

About Paramita

Paramita Roy is a Hindustani classical and contemporary vocalist, and a teacher at Artium. She releases through the Teacher Series, and she designed the artwork for this record herself.

Her students Sreeja Mukherjee, Mrinalini Sengupta and Trisha Maiti sing the chorus on this record; her own Music Theory teacher, Dr. Bappa Dutta, wrote and composed it. “To me, the phrase is an invitation rather than an instruction — it is almost like gently reminding someone to take a moment, turn inward and remember Hari.”

Paramita Roy, tracking vocals

A Note from the Talent Development Department

The detail I keep coming back to on this one is the chorus. Paramita is singing a song about which companions stay when things get hard — and standing behind her, singing it with her, are three of her own students. Sreeja, Mrinalini and Trisha did not get put on the track to fill it out. She put them there.

There is a second version of the same thing running underneath it: the man who wrote and composed the song is her own Music Theory teacher. So the record has a teacher on both sides of her — one she learns from, three who learn from her. For a Teacher Series release, that is about as complete a statement of the thing as you could ask for.
