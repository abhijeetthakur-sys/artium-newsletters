One voice, one microphone, and a 2007 film song rebuilt into something closer to rock.

  ✦  

Inaya Sharma chose a Shreya Ghoshal song — Ye Ishq Hai, from Jab We Met — and she is precise about why. "It is a very happy and lively song," she says. "This song has a lot of expressions, and it teaches me that I have to enjoy music."

Then unMute took it apart. Akshay Purandare produced and arranged the version she would actually sing, and Abhijeet Thakur wrote and sang the sargam section running through it. That section is the part she loved most. "This new version was pretty fun and better than the original karaoke. It felt like a rock song with those sargams, and I love rock songs."

Ask her what she was thinking as she walked on and she does not describe a feeling. She quotes herself a rule: those who keep trying never truly fail. She is equally level about nerves. "In some hard performances, I keep thinking that I will do this wrong, that wrong, but I don't."

In the seconds before the first line, three things were in her head — her teachers, Smriti Minocha and Abhishek Marotkar, her parents, and Shreya Ghoshal. Or as she puts it, "the singer who is the original king or queen of the song."

The note she carried onto the stage came out of rehearsal, from Abhijeet and JD: enjoy the song, sing it with expression, but never miss the sur or the taal. She has clearly turned it over since. "A song looks very easy, but if you dive deep into the song, then you will know that the song is hard."

And the walk off: "I looked at my parents' faces, the audience's faces, and the people who were with me. I had a sense of achievement." Her mother was out there in the audience with her phone up, filming the whole thing.

A note from the Talent Department

We handed Inaya a version of Ye Ishq Hai that was not the one she had learned. Akshay rebuilt the track, and I wrote a sargam section into it and sang it. That is a lot to put in front of a young student who has spent months getting comfortable with the original.

She did not just cope with it. She picked the sargams as her favourite part. "It felt like a rock song with those sargams, and I love rock songs." That is not a student adapting. That is a performer with taste.

The line of hers I keep coming back to is this one: "A song looks very easy, but if you dive deep into the song, then you will know that the song is hard." Plenty of people sing for years and never arrive at that sentence. She got there somewhere between rehearsal and the stage, and she said it without making a thing of it.

Credit where it belongs — to her teachers Smriti Minocha & Abhishek Marotkar, and to her parents, one of them with a phone held up the whole way through. She thought about all of them before she sang a note.

unMute does not wait for an artist to be famous before giving them a proper stage. That is the whole point.

Watch it. Share it if it moves you.

Abhijeet Thakur

About artium unMute

unMute is Artium Academy's live performance series, built as a format, not a one-off event. It's a recurring show with its own sound, its own city editions, and a roster of artists who return season after season.

Each episode is shot and produced to a fixed format, city by city, so the show scales without losing what makes it work: real performances, minimal gloss, teachers and students sharing the same stage.

unMute is where Artium's talent gets a platform beyond the classroom, and where the audience gets to watch music get made, not just performed.
